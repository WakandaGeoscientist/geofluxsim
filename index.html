Start here: how GeoFluxSim works

GeoFluxSim simulates a turbidity current: water carrying suspended sediment that is denser than the water around it, so it runs downhill along the floor of a flume or tank, then drops its sediment as a deposit. You set up the experiment, run it, and read what the current did and what it left.

  1. Choose an experiment in Setup: a ready-made scenario, or the solver (Experiment) and how the current is released.
  2. Set the sediment: how much there is, its grain types, sizes and densities, and any mud.
  3. Set the flow and flume: slope, inflow speed and thickness, run time.
  4. Press Play. Step moves one frame; Rewind goes back to any earlier moment.
  5. Read the results below the 3D view: deposits, flow at the probe, how grains move, eddies, data, and a shareable video.
  6. Test an idea: pin runs to compare them, or let a range study vary one input for you.

Two-layer flow

The current starts as one dense layer. Mixing at its top sheds fluid and fine grains into a dilute layer above, and grains settle back down. Where the basal layer gets dense enough, grain friction slows it like a traction carpet, and where it stalls it freezes onto the bed.

Mud (flocculated clay)

Add several mud types to one run: kaolinite and chlorite (weakly cohesive), illite (moderately), bentonite or smectite (strongly), carbonate (lime) mud, or non-cohesive silt-like mud. Their gel strengths combine as Baker et al. (2025) found for clay mixtures, so even a modest share of strongly cohesive clay can dominate. Illite, chlorite and carbonate mud values are estimates. Mud rides along as flocs that settle very slowly. Flocs take up far more room than their solid clay, so they crowd the sand and slow its settling, and they make the water the sand falls through denser. Both keep sand in suspension for longer. Floc density sets how much room the flocs take: the lower it is, the stronger the effect.

The current always erodes its own fresh deposit as well as the starting bed, youngest sediment first. Fresh-deposit erodibility makes newly laid, loosely packed sediment easier to pick up than older bed; the boost fades over the settling-in time. 1× treats fresh and old sediment the same.

What the AVO says

How AVO works, in plain words

When a seismic wave meets a boundary between two rocks, part of it reflects. How much depends on the change in acoustic impedance (velocity × density) across the boundary, but also on the angle at which the wave arrives: that dependence is AVO (amplitude versus offset, or angle).

Two numbers describe it. The intercept A is the reflection straight down (zero angle): positive (a peak) where the rock below is harder, negative (a trough) where it is softer. The gradient B is how fast the reflection changes with angle; it depends mostly on the change in shear-wave velocity, which fluids barely affect but rock stiffness does.

Gas in a sandstone's pores makes it much softer to compression but not to shear, so it lowers A and pushes B more negative. That is why fluids are judged from how reflections change between near and far angle stacks rather than from a single full stack.

AVO classes (Rutherford & Williams, 1989; Castagna et al., 1998), for the top of a sand under shale: class I, hard sand (peak, dimming with angle); class II and IIp, little contrast (weak, may reverse polarity, brightens on far stacks); class III, soft sand (strong trough, brightening: the bright spot); class IV, soft sand under a stiff cap (strong trough, dimming). On the crossplot, brine-filled rocks fall near the background trend; gas sands move away from it, down and to the left.

Here velocities and densities come from typical values for each rock and its burial, shear velocities from Castagna's relations, fluid changes from Gassmann's equation, and angles from Shuey's approximation. It shows the principles; real data add noise, anisotropy and processing effects.

Computed by the model

Flow thickness, velocity and sediment concentration (layer-averaged in the 2D flume and Quick tank; cell by cell in the 2D slice and Full 3D); settling, entrainment, bedload and deposition for every grain class; mud flocculation and cohesion; grain-to-grain stresses; the bed, its layers and stratigraphy; Froude, Reynolds, Richardson, Shields and Bagnold numbers; every chart, table and study result.

Illustrated only

Grain positions in the grain view and near-bed animation; the cloud texture in the realistic 3D view; outcrop colours and weathering; the photo reading on your device. The grain-scale (DEM) view is computed but does not feed back into the current. Outcrop and seismic views scale the flume deposit up uniformly, which real basins don't do.

Checked against known answers

Mass conservation (every mode, every run); lock-exchange front speed and depth against Shin et al. (2004); settling against Stokes' law. Run them in Studies, Benchmarks. Not yet checked against: lobe dimensions, carbonate grain settling, mud-rich flow transformations, minibasins, or self-acceleration.

Main assumptions

Turbidity currents: shallow-water (layer-averaged) equations with Parker et al. (1986) entrainment, Garcia & Parker (1993) bed erosion and a front condition; Boussinesq Navier–Stokes with a Smagorinsky closure in the 2D slice and Full 3D. Laboratory scale: results scale to nature only qualitatively. Values marked "estimated" (illite, chlorite, carbonate mud cohesion) are not from measurements.

Reproducibility

Runs are deterministic: the same settings give the same result. Saved reports, study exports and shared links record the settings and the app build (GeoFluxSim with David, build 2026-11).

How the model works

The current is a layer-averaged gravity flow (Parker et al., 1986). It carries six grain classes, siliciclastic and carbonate, each split into fine, median and coarse sizes set by the sorting, plus an optional mud class. It entrains ambient water, is driven downslope by its excess density, and loses momentum to bed friction. Each class settles at its own Ferguson & Church (2004) velocity, using the grain density, shape constants and the water's viscosity at the chosen temperature, with Richardson–Zaki hindered settling at high concentration.

Mud is carried as flocs. They settle at the floc velocity you set, not at a velocity from their 4 µm clay size, and the Garcia & Parker formula barely re-entrains them. Flocs are mostly water, so each unit of clay fills (ρm − ρa)/(ρfloc − ρa) units of volume. That floc volume is added to the sand's in the hindered-settling term, as for sand settling through a mud suspension (Winterwerp, 2002; te Slaa et al., 2015). The mud also raises the density of the fluid the sand falls through, which reduces the sand's excess weight. Both effects use the near-bed mud concentration. Damping of turbulence and gelling of very muddy flows are not modelled.

In the 2D flume the current can run as two layers, after the bipartite flows seen in experiments and in high-density turbidites. A dense basal layer lies next to the bed under a dilute turbulent layer, and each has its own thickness, velocity and six-class load. The basal layer feels the weight of the layer above, and the dilute layer rides on the basal layer's surface. Mixing at the interface lifts basal fluid and grains into the dilute layer at a rate set by the interface Richardson number, using the same law as ambient entrainment with its own coefficient. Interfacial drag pulls the two velocities together, and only the dilute layer entrains ambient water. Grains settle from the dilute layer onto the basal layer, or straight to the bed where there is no basal layer, and the basal layer deposits with its own near-bed ratio. Where its near-bed concentration passes 12–35 vol %, a Coulomb grain friction on the grains' immersed weight slows it, like a traction carpet. If a carpet stalls, the basal layer freezes onto the bed at once. How far sediment travels depends mostly on the basal near-bed ratio, so compare runs across it with the range study.

The current can enter as an inflow or be released from a lock: a column of suspension behind a gate at the upstream end, let go at time zero into still water. The bed can have a slope break, with one gradient upstream and another downstream; Full 3D treats the break as a change in the downslope pull of gravity.

In the 2D flume and Quick tank, the still water ahead of the current pushes back on the head's forward face with its stagnation pressure, ½ ρ u² for each metre of face height, scaled by 1/Frf² and spread over the face from the head's crest to its tip. Without it, a layer-averaged current runs into still water far too fast. With the front Froude number at 1, a lock release reproduces the energy-conserving front speed of Shin, Dalziel & Linden (2004); check it with the Benchmarks panel. Set it to 0 to turn the front condition off.

Events can be stacked. "Next event on this deposit" starts a new current on the bed left by the earlier ones, keeping its relief, grain sorting, fresh-deposit state (settled for the time between events) and stratigraphy, so the bed steers and feeds the new current. In the deposit section the darkest lines mark where each event began. Rewind uses whole-model snapshots taken through the run (in Full 3D and the 2D slice, of the full velocity and sediment fields), so going back and pressing Play continues exactly from that moment. Tap the 3D view to read the flow and the bed at that point, or tap the Along the flume chart to move the probe there.

Each sediment population can be a different material: picking one sets its density (effective density for porous or hollow grains such as forams and pumice) and shape together, and changing any of these makes it Custom. Settling follows either Ferguson & Church (2004), with shape constants, or Dietrich (1982), from the Corey shape factor and Powers roundness, which captures flat, elongate and angular grains such as mica, shell plates and skeletal fragments. Colours are for display only.

Grains move in four ways. Each sand class at each point of the bed has a Shields number from the bed shear velocity, u* = √Cd |U|, and a threshold of motion from Soulsby & Whitehouse (1997) for the surface layer's mean size, with a hiding function so that fine grains among coarse ones are sheltered and coarse ones exposed. Below threshold they rest. Above it, the share of moving grains that goes into suspension rises as u* overtakes the settling velocity, following van Rijn's (1984) criterion, and that load is the entrainment already described. The rest move as bedload: mostly rolling and sliding up to about twice the threshold, mostly saltating above it, with hop height, length and speed from Sklar & Dietrich (2004). Bedload flux uses Meyer-Peter & Müller as corrected by Wong & Parker (2006), class by class from the surface layer, and moves the bed through an Exner balance; bedload leaving the downstream end counts as bypassed. Mud flocs travel only in suspension. Bed slope does not yet change the threshold. In the layer-averaged modes u* comes from the layer's mean velocity; in Full 3D from the cell next to the bed. The near-bed view draws grains at true scale with these computed shares and hop sizes, but the paths of individual grains are illustrative.

The 2D slice runs the Navier–Stokes solver of Full 3D on one cell across the flume, which lets the grid be fine enough along and up the flow (down to about 0.6 cm by 0.4 cm) to resolve Kelvin–Helmholtz billows on the current's upper shear layer, the lobes and clefts of the head, and the eddies shed behind it. Nothing about them is prescribed: they grow from the computed shear and are damped by the computed density stratification. The Eddies section shows the centre plane of the 2D slice or Full 3D run: concentration, velocity, spanwise vorticity and the gradient Richardson number, with Ri < 1/4 marking where the Miles–Howard criterion allows shear instability. It also measures the billow wavelength from the interface and compares it with the fastest-growing wavelength of a tanh shear layer, about 7.1 times its vorticity thickness (Michalke, 1964). In two dimensions eddies cannot stretch and break into three-dimensional turbulence, so billows persist longer and mix differently than in nature or in Full 3D.

Grain-to-grain interactions are included in every mode through Bagnold's (1954) grain-stress law. Near the bed, grains sheared past each other either push through the fluid between them (macro-viscous regime) or collide (grain-inertia regime), depending on the Bagnold number. Their linear concentration λ measures how close they are, rising steeply as the concentration approaches the packing limit. The resulting grain stress adds resistance to the current, and the dispersive pressure that goes with it holds grains up against settling, more for larger grains, slowing their deposition. In the 2D slice and Full 3D the suspension also thickens with concentration (Krieger & Dougherty, 1959). The two-layer flow keeps its Coulomb friction for a locked traction carpet. A grain-scale model (discrete elements) at the probe resolves the grains themselves (springs, dashpots, Coulomb friction and rotation, with fluid drag from the computed near-bed flow) to show collisions, contact force chains and segregation; it is driven by the current but doesn't feed back into it.

Mud can be cohesive, with a clay type: kaolinite (weakly cohesive), illite (moderately cohesive; its values are interpolated, not measured), bentonite or smectite (strongly cohesive), or a kaolinite–bentonite mixture. The gel's yield stress follows Baker et al.'s (2017) fits to lock-exchange flows and rheometer tests, τy = 37.9 Pa (C/Cm)³ up to Cm = 22% (kaolinite) or 16% (bentonite), rising to 271 Pa at 30.5% or 20.5%. Mixtures use Baker et al.'s (2025) combination of the linear and non-linear forms, which reproduced their measured yield stresses. Turbulence breaks bonds in a moving current, so the gel acts with τy²/(τy + ρu*²). It resists the flow at the bed, damps the turbulent mixing of water into the current, holds up sand grains it is strong enough to support (yield number above about 0.05; Beris et al., 1985), and when a gel-dominated current slows almost to rest it freezes en masse (Mulder & Alexander, 2001). The experiments were small lock-exchange flows; natural currents are faster and more turbulent, so they need more clay for the same effect.

Velocity and concentration inside the current are rebuilt from the layer state. Velocity peaks at 20% of the current thickness, with a power law below and a Gaussian decay above (after Altinakar et al., 1996). Concentration decays exponentially upward from a near-bed value set by the near-bed concentration ratio. In the Concentration and Velocity views, the near half of the current is drawn as coloured fog and the centre line of the flume is cut open as a coloured slice, with contour lines and the profile at the probe drawn on it in white.

Settled sediment is stored in 1.5-second layers, so the deposit keeps its internal stratigraphy. Dark lines every 7.5 s are time lines. Deposit colours are stretched to the range present in the deposit.

The current can pick its own deposit back up. Each class is entrained at the Garcia & Parker (1993) rate, scaled by its share of a 2 mm active layer at the bed surface (Hirano, 1971). Erosion removes the youngest sediment first, so eroded surfaces truncate the stratigraphy, and the current re-erodes its own deposit from the same run as readily as the starting bed. Optionally, newly laid sediment is more erodible: the rate is multiplied by 1 + (Kf − 1) f, where f is the share of the 2 mm active layer that is fresh. Each column tracks how much fresh sediment it holds: deposition adds to it, erosion removes it first, and it settles in, decaying over the time Tc. The starting bed counts as settled. Set bed erodibility to 0 to turn this off. The local bed slope includes the deposit's own surface, and hindered settling uses the near-bed concentration.

The 2D flume solves the current along the flume, per unit width, with the vertical structure rebuilt from the layer. The 3D tank solves the same layer equations across the floor, in both downstream and cross-stream directions, so the current can spread from an inlet narrower than the tank. The side walls are closed and the far end drains. Its vertical structure is rebuilt the same way, so it doesn't resolve 3D turbulence or a lobe's internal circulation, and the coarser grid adds some numerical spreading.

Full 3D is a different solver. It solves the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with the Boussinesq approximation on a staggered grid, so velocity and every grain class are computed in every cell along, across and up through the water. Nothing is assumed about the velocity or concentration profile, and mixing with the water above comes from the computed flow instead of an entrainment formula. Pressure is solved exactly each step with a fast cosine transform. Turbulence smaller than a grid cell uses a Smagorinsky large-eddy model, the bed uses the quadratic drag Cd, and the bed slope tilts gravity. Grains settle and enter or leave the bed through the bottom cell. In this mode the entrainment coefficient and near-bed ratio are not used.

With a starting bed and bed change speed-up, the bed becomes terrain in Full 3D. Cells below its surface are solid, the flow goes around and over them, and sediment settles and erodes at the surface, so scours and lobes steer the current. In the depth-averaged modes the same relief acts through its slope.

In the Realistic view, the largest shapes and where the cloud sits come from the computed field. The fine billows at its edge and the grain texture on the bed are added for display and aren't computed. Use the Concentration and Velocity views to read the computed values.

Full 3D limits: browser grids are coarse, so large eddies are computed while smaller ones are modelled, and the true Reynolds number is not fully resolved. Terrain follows the grid in steps, so features smaller than a cell don't affect the flow, and particles don't feed back on the fluid except through density.

Limits of the 2D flume and Quick tank: these are dilute, depth-averaged models with a fixed flume. It leaves out bedload, flocculation, cohesion and side-wall effects, and the starting bed has one uniform mix. Its coefficients have not been calibrated against your own experiments, so treat results as a guide until you have compared them with measurements.
